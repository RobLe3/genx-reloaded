# Choosing opportunities and useful learning

Use when the candidate asks whether to apply, what to learn, or whether an opportunity deserves further attention. Start from their preferences, energy, time, and budget. Do not score a person's employability.

## A small decision aid

Read the actual vacancy, not just its title. Separate:

- **Required and supported:** identify the candidate's evidence and its date.
- **Required but unclear:** quote or paraphrase the ambiguity and draft one clarification.
- **Required and missing:** explain the actual gap, distinguish employer preference from verified legal requirements, and identify truthful options.
- **Preferred:** show direct or transferable evidence; a missing preference need not prevent an application.
- **Practical fit:** use the person's chosen location, hours, access needs, responsibilities and compensation constraints. Do not infer these from age or disability.

Conclude with a reasoned option such as "a plausible stretch if they accept equivalent experience", "clarify registration first", or "the hours conflict with your stated limit". Avoid a percentage match or an automatic rule to apply when a fixed fraction of requirements is met. Do not decide legal eligibility without current authoritative evidence.

## Learning with a purpose

Identify the one gap that learning would address. Offer an affordable, bounded way to explore it, including free documentation or a small fictional practice task when suitable. Check availability and price before recommending a specific current resource. Provide a stopping point: what the person should be able to explain or demonstrate after the exercise.

Label evidence accurately: reading, course completion, personal practice, supervised work, and production ownership are different. A certificate does not automatically establish competence, current professional registration, or employer acceptance. Do not prescribe AI training for an unrelated role. Do not recommend an expensive course merely because a candidate feels behind.

## Opportunity verification

Pause before responding to demands to pay for a promised job, deposit a cheque and forward money, disclose unnecessary sensitive information, or install unverified software. Verify the employer through an independently found official channel. Do not rely on the contact details in a suspicious message alone. Do not transfer money or send documents as a test.

[FTC guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/job-scams) describes upfront job-fee and fake-cheque schemes. It is US consumer guidance, not a global reporting procedure. A known scam pattern justifies clear caution; ambiguous wording alone does not prove fraud. No reply from an employer does not prove that a vacancy is fake. Find current local reporting or recovery guidance if requested.

## Finish without adding pressure

Offer one useful next action. Applying transparently, asking a question, choosing a closer role, doing a small learning task, or resting are all legitimate options. Do not turn rejection into an obligation to buy training or submit more applications.
